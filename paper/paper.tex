\begin{abstract}
  In an exploration of building simplicial and cubical set models in indexed form, we extend a previous construction of semi--simplicial and semi--cubical sets with degeneracies. The degeneracies we construct can be characterized as adding a new dimension, and coincide with usual degeneracies in the cubical set case. As necessitated by the construction, we refined the degeneracies and coherence conditions outlined in Grandis--Mauri~\cite{grandis03} to make it step-wise. Our work includes a mechanically checked formalization in Rocq.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\subsection{Motivation}
Semi--simplicial sets are usually defined as presheaves from the semi--simplicial category, termed as the \emph{fibred} form~\cite{rourkesanderson71}. In our previous work, we proposed a uniform construction of semi--simplicial and semi--cubical sets using iterated parametericity, termed as the \emph{indexed} form~\cite{hr25}, using the \emph{fibred-indexed correspondence}. That our indexed construction is equivalent to the fibred one is proved in~\cite{Laroche26}. It was originally suggested at Univalent Foundations IAS 2012~\cite{UFwiki2012} that the open problem of constructing semi--simplicial \emph{types} could be solved with the indexed construction, as it supplies more definitional equalities for tackling the combinatorial problem of solving coherence conditions~\footnote{It was originally postulated that the coherences would come for free with the indexed construction, but this turns out not to be the case.}.

\subsection{Contributions}
We previously exploited the observation that semi--simplicial and semi--cubical sets are naturally unary and binary cases of iterated parametricity~\cite{hr25}. We extend this construction with operations that insert a new coordinate axis: the usual cubical degeneracies in the binary case and their unary analogue~\cite{bernardy15,bernardy12}. The coherence conditions we used to complete our construction made explicit the ones outlined in Grandis--Mauri~\cite{grandis03}. Our work includes a mechanically-checked formalization in Rocq~\href{https://github.com/artagnon/bonak}{\linkicon}, including a proof of equivalence with Grandis--Mauri's definition of cubical sets with degeneracies~\href{https://github.com/artagnon/bonak/tree/master/theories/Equiv}{\linkicon}.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$\nu$}{nu}-shape sets}

Fix a set $\nu$ of face labels, called the \emph{arity}. The unary case uses a singleton set of labels; the binary case uses two labels. The augmented semi--simplicial and semi--cubical categories are instances of the more general notion of $\nu$--semi--shape category.

\begin{definition}[$\hexagon$]
  The definition of $\nu$--semi--shape category is shown below. Note that, if $g \circ f$ is well--defined, then the length of $f$ is less than or equal to that of $g$. A star denotes a retained coordinate and a label in $\nu$ denotes an inserted constant. We write $|l|_{\kstar}$ for the number of stars in a word $l$ and $\epsilon$ for the empty word. Its objects, morphisms, and identities are:
  \begin{align*}
    \Obj_{\hexagon}       & := \Nat                                                                       \\
    \Hom_{\hexagon}(p, n) & := \{l \in (\nu \sqcup \{\kstar\})^n \mid |l|_{\kstar} = p\}                  \\
    g \circ f             & :=
    \begin{cases}
      f                & \text{if $g = \epsilon$}                                                   \\
      a\,(g' \circ f)  & \text{if $g = a\,g'$}, \text{where $a \in \nu$}                            \\
      a\,(g' \circ f') & \text{if $g = \kstar\,g'$, $f = a\,f'$, where $a \in \nu$ or $a = \kstar$} \\
    \end{cases}       \\
    \id                   & := \kstar \ldots \kstar \text{ $n$ times for $\id \in \Hom_{\hexagon}(n, n)$}
  \end{align*}
\end{definition}

In particular, the $1$--semi--shape category presents the augmented semi--simplicial category while the $2$--semi--shape category presents the semi--cubical category (note that this also relates to unary and binary parametricity~\cite{bernardy15, reynolds83}).

Degeneracies can be added to the $\nu$--semi--shape category in different ways. We here focus on cubical--style degeneracies as in Grandis~\cite{grandis03} and their adaptation to the unary case as in \cite{bernardy15}. A morphism from $p$ to $n$ in the $\nu$--shape category retains $k$ of the $p$ input coordinates: it first discards the other $p-k$ coordinates, then inserts constants to obtain $n$ output coordinates.

\begin{definition}[$\hexagon_d$]
  The $\nu$--shape category extends the $\nu$--semi--shape category with degeneracies. We write $|w|_{\kstar}$ for the number of stars in a word $w$. Its objects, morphisms, and identities are:
  \begin{align*}
    \Obj_{\hexagon_d}      & := \Nat                                  \\
    \Hom_{\hexagon_d}(p,n) & := \{(r,f) \in \{\kstar,\bullet\}^p
    \times (\nu \sqcup \{\kstar\})^n \mid |r|_{\kstar}=|f|_{\kstar}\} \\
    \id_n                  & := (\kstar^n,\kstar^n).
  \end{align*}
  In the selection mask $r$, a star retains an input coordinate and a bullet discards it. To define composition, first normalize a face word followed by a selection mask. For words $f$ and $s$ of the same length, with $k$ and $l$ stars respectively, define $\mathsf{cross}(f,s)\in\Hom_{\hexagon_d}(k,l)$ recursively by
  \begin{align*}
    \mathsf{cross}(\epsilon,\epsilon)  & := (\epsilon,\epsilon), \\
    \mathsf{cross}(a f,\bullet s)      & := (t,h),               \\
    \mathsf{cross}(a f,\kstar s)       & := (t,a h),             \\
    \mathsf{cross}(\kstar f,\bullet s) & := (\bullet t,h),       \\
    \mathsf{cross}(\kstar f,\kstar s)  & := (\kstar t,\kstar h),
  \end{align*}
  where $\epsilon$ is the empty word, $a\in\nu$, and $(t,h)=\mathsf{cross}(f,s)$ in each recursive clause. Thus a discarded constant disappears, a retained constant remains in the output word, and an input variable is retained exactly when the mask retains its position.

  For $(r,f):p\to m$ and $(s,g):m\to n$, composition is
  \[
    (s,g)\circ_{\hexagon_d}(r,f)
    := (r\circ_{\hexagon_1}t,\;g\circ_{\hexagon}h)
    \quad\text{where }(t,h)=\mathsf{cross}(f,s).
  \]
  Here $\circ_{\hexagon_1}$ is the semi--shape word composition with $\bullet$ as its sole label: it substitutes $t$ into the stars of $r$. The other component substitutes $h$ into the stars of $g$. These operations satisfy the identity and associativity laws.
\end{definition}

In particular, a $2$--shape category presents the cubical category, while $1$--shape category presents the simplicial category with cubical--style degeneracies, which we explain in more detail in section \ref{sec:dgn-flavors}.

\subsection{Fibred-indexed correspondence}

We now work in dependent type theory. A type $A$ is an \emph{h--set} if, for any $x,y:A$ and any two proofs $p,q:x=y$, we have $p=q$. Thus equality between elements of $A$ is proof-irrelevant: this is the uniqueness of identity proofs property ($\UIP$). We write $\HSet$ for the universe of h--sets.

Semi--cubical sets can be expressed directly in terms of presheaves over a $\nu$--semi--shape category using morphisms, by iterating the equivalence between maps and families:
\[
  (\Sigma A : \HSet. A \rightarrow B) \simeq (B \rightarrow \HSet)
\]

\section{Our construction}
\subsection{Intuition for our construction}
We call the structures presented by presheaves on the $\nu$--semi--shape category \emph{$\nu$--sets}, and write $\X$ for the type of their indexed presentations.

We call a degeneracy that inserts a new coordinate axis a \emph{parametric degeneracy}. A degeneracy direction specifies where a new coordinate axis is inserted. For an $n$-cube, there are $n+1$ insertion positions, numbered $0,\ldots,n$.

We recall the boundary terminology of the indexed construction~\cite{hr25}. A \emph{layer} groups the face data for one direction, with one component for each label in $\nu$. A \emph{frame} records a prefix of these boundary layers; a \emph{full frame} contains all of them. A \emph{painting} over a frame supplies the remaining boundary layers and a cell over the completed boundary. In the dependent presentation, each component of a layer is itself a lower-dimensional painting over a suitable restricted frame. Restriction means taking a face, and acts on frames, layers, and paintings.

The boundary of a degenerate cell contains a repeated layer at the inserted direction, together with degeneracies of the original faces. Figure~\ref{fig:degeneracy-layers} illustrates the three insertion positions for a square.

\subsubsection{Parametric degeneracies vs connecting degeneracies}
\label{sec:dgn-flavors}

We use $n$-shape to mean a cell of geometric dimension $n$. Despite a common terminology, degeneracies in simplicial sets~\cite{fri08} and in cubical sets~\cite{grandis03} are of a different nature. Degeneracies in cubical sets insert a new axis while degeneracies in simplicial sets duplicate an existing axis. In particular, degenerating a $n$-shape $c$ in a cubical set is about \emph{inserting a new axis} $i \leq n$ (counting axes of a $n$-shape from $0$ to $n-1$), yielding a $(n+1)$-shape lying on $c$ in the $i$-th axis and lying on the degeneracy of the $j$-th axis of $c$ in the $j$-th axis (resp. $(j-1)$-th axis) for $j < i$ (resp $j > i$). In the simplicial case, the degeneracy process is better understood by reasoning in augmented simplicial sets where the dimension starts at $-1$: degenerating a $n$-shape $c$ in simplicial sets is about \emph{duplicating an existing axis} $i < n$ (thus requiring $n$ to be at least $0$), yielding a $(n+1)$-shape lying on $c$ in both the $i$ and $i+1$ axes, while it lies again on the degeneracy of the $j$-th axis (resp. $(j-1)$-th) of $c$ in the $j$-th axis for $j < i$ (resp. $j > i+1$).

We call the axis-duplicating operation a \emph{connecting degeneracy}; the axis-inserting operation is the parametric degeneracy introduced above. The unary case of parametric degeneracies have already been considered in the literature, e.g. implicitly in type theories with internal parametricity~\cite{moulin16,bernardy12} and more explicitly in Parametric Type Theory~\cite{bernardy15} where an interpretation in terms of a unary form of cubical sets is given. The binary case of connecting degeneracies happens to be similar to connections in (binary) cubical sets, where it should be noted that for arity 2 (and higher), connections additionally need parametric degeneracies to fill faces which in the non-duplicated axes. It should also be noted that each connection has one variant for each face label in $\nu$ (that is only one variant in the unary case but two in the binary cases, resulting in the ``disjunctive'' and ``conjunctive'' variants).

\subsubsection{Construction of faces}
\label{sec:faces}

The construction of degeneracies presented in this paper builds on the very dependent recursive presentation~\cite{hr26} of the indexed construction of $\nu$--sets~\cite{hr25}. At each level $n$, the earlier families $X_0,\ldots,X_{n-1}$ determine the type of full frames, and the next family $X_n$ assigns an h--set of cells to each full frame.

In the formulas below, $n$ is the construction level and $p\leq n$ is the number of layers in a prefix, called its \emph{height}. A full frame at level $n$ has $n$ layers. Thus level $n$ corresponds to geometric dimension $n-1$ in the augmented unary case and to dimension $n$ in the binary case. We write $\framep[n][p]$ for frames of height $p$, $\layer[n][p][][d=d]$ for the next layer over a frame $d$, and $\painting[n][p][][d=d]$ for its paintings. In particular, $\fullframe[n]=\framep[n][n]$, and a painting over a full frame $d$ is a cell of $X_n(d)$. A successor frame is a dependent pair $(d,l)$ of a frame and its next layer; a painting with layers left to supply is a pair $(l,c)$ of its next layer and remaining painting.

The restriction operation $\restrf[n][p][][q][\epsilon]$ maps a frame in $\framep[n+1][p]$ to one in $\framep[n][p]$. Its relative index $q\leq n-p$ denotes direction $p+q$, and $\epsilon\in\nu$ selects a face in that direction. The corresponding layer and painting operations are written $\restrl$ and $\restrp$; a painting restriction includes the restriction of its indexing frame.

Restrictions follow the decomposition into frames, layers, and paintings. Restricting a successor frame restricts its prefix and its next layer; restricting a layer applies painting restriction to each face-label component. On a painting $(l,c)$, restriction at index $0$ selects the component $l(\epsilon)$ of the first layer. A successor index restricts the layer and continues recursively through the remaining painting.

\subsubsection{Construction of degeneracies}

We construct degeneracies by building their boundaries layer by layer. The chosen direction may lie beyond the prefix, or the prefix may have reached or passed it. These two cases give the operations \emph{Below} and \emph{Above}, written $\downarrow$ and $\uparrow$. Their direction indices are relative to the prefix height: Below counts upwards, so its index $q$ denotes direction $p+q$; Above counts downwards, so its index $q$ denotes direction $p-q$.

Below transforms the existing prefix without inserting the repeated layer. It acts on a frame alone and preserves the prefix height:
\begin{equation*}
  \reflfbelow[n][p][][q]:\framep[n][p]\rightarrow\framep[n+1][p]
  \qquad(q\leq n-p).
\end{equation*}
Above inserts the repeated layer, so it needs the painting $c$ completing the original prefix and produces one more layer:
\begin{equation*}
  \reflfabove[n][p][][q]:\Pi d:\framep[n][p].\,
  \painting[n][p][][d=\d]\rightarrow\framep[n+1][p+1]
  \qquad(q\leq p).
\end{equation*}

For any $(X_0,X_1,\ldots):\X$, let $R$ collect the degeneracy families at levels below $n$. The supplied family $L$ at level $n$ has type
\begin{equation*}
  L:\Pi q\leq n.\,\Pi d:\fullframe[n].\,\Pi x:X_n(d).\,
  X_{n+1}(\reflfabove[n][n][][q][d=\d,c=x]).
\end{equation*}
Here $q$ is the Above index, so $L(q)$ inserts direction $n-q$. In the geometric examples, this operation is denoted $r^n_{n-q}$ in the binary case and $r^{n-1}_{n-q-1}$ in the augmented unary case. The underlying $X_i$ and earlier degeneracies $R$ are left implicit.

\subsubsection{Coherence conditions}
\label{sec:coherences}

The five relations of Grandis--Mauri~\cite{grandis03} describe commutation of restrictions and degeneracies, and cancellation of a degeneracy by a restriction in its inserted direction. Grandis--Mauri state the relations between maps in the cubical site. Here we display their images under a contravariant presheaf, which reverses composition. Thus $\delta_r^\alpha$ denotes restriction on cells and $\varepsilon_q$ denotes degeneracy, with face labels $\alpha,\beta\in\nu$. Composition is read from right to left, and dimension indices are left implicit:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{array}{r@{\;}c@{\;}c@{}l@{\qquad}l@{\qquad}l}
    \delta_q^\alpha\delta_r^\beta     & =       &                     &
    \delta_r^\beta\delta_{q+1}^\alpha & r\leq q & (\relRestrRestr),                                                                  \\[0.4em]
                                      &         & \ldelim\{{3}{*}     &
    \varepsilon_{q-1}\delta_r^\alpha  & r<q     & (\relRestrReflInf),                                                                \\
    \delta_r^\alpha\varepsilon_q      & =       &                     &
    \id                               & r=q     & (\relRestrReflId),                                                                 \\
                                      &         &                     & \varepsilon_q\delta_{r-1}^\alpha & r>q & (\relRestrReflSup), \\[0.4em]
    \varepsilon_q\varepsilon_r        & =       &                     &
    \varepsilon_{r+1}\varepsilon_q    & q\leq r & (\relReflRefl).
  \end{array}
\end{equation}
These are zero-based absolute direction indices, ranging over the instances in which the composites are defined. At level $n$, the cell component of $\varepsilon_i$ is supplied by $L(n-i)$. The three cases distinguish a restriction before, at, or after the inserted direction.

The mixed relation above is displayed in the simplifying direction. In the construction tables, the equality proofs are oriented in the direction used for transport, as in the formalization. Coherence names list the operations on the right-hand side of those construction-oriented equations, with the outer operation first. In particular, $\relRestrReflBelowId$ proves $d=\cRF{q}{\epsilon}{\cSF{q}{d}}$, reversing the cancellation equation above.

In the indexed construction, these relations become equalities of partial boundaries. The Below/Above distinction refines $\relRestrReflSup$ into $\relRestrReflBelowSup$ and $\relRestrReflAboveSup$, and $\relReflRefl$ into $\relReflBelowBelow$, $\relReflBelowAbove$, and $\relReflAboveAbove$. Together with $\relRestrReflBelowInf$ and $\relRestrReflBelowId$, these are the seven degeneracy coherence families. The underlying $\nu$--set already supplies $\relRestrRestr$.

Typing the layer operations requires transporting paintings along frame equalities. The Below layer step uses $\relRestrReflBelowInf$ to commute degeneracy with restriction. At Above index $0$, $\relRestrReflBelowId$ identifies the restriction of the degenerate frame with the original frame, allowing the input painting to form a constant layer. At a successor Above index, the layer step uses $\relRestrReflAboveSup$. The restriction layer similarly uses $\relRestrRestr$ to commute two restrictions. The frame operations and the types of the coherences they require are therefore constructed together.

Tables~\ref{tab:cohframe-types} and~\ref{tab:cohframe-dgn} give their types for frames. We assume $p\leq n$ and face labels $\epsilon,\omega\in\nu$. Each row quantifies over its displayed arguments and bounds, using the relative direction indices introduced above. Operation superscripts are omitted where the dimensions follow from the argument types. Indexing frames of layer and painting operations are implicit; an Above frame operation displays both its frame and painting arguments. The resulting equality is between frames even when its terms involve paintings.

In the tables, transports and the standard conversions between equalities of dependent pairs or functions and their componentwise equalities are left implicit, as if working in extensional type theory. The Rocq formalization makes these conversions explicit. The intensional justification of the layer proof also uses a second coherence, an equality between composites of frame--equality proofs. Besides the lower-dimensional painting coherence, it requires those composites to agree so that the implicit transports have the same effect. Since frames are sets, this second coherence follows from uniqueness of identity proofs ($\UIP$). It does not replace the painting coherence: the two are separate premises of the layer argument.

The families use this recursive decomposition, with different dependencies and boundary cases. The families $\relRestrReflBelowInf$ and $\relRestrReflBelowId$ each have a single Below stage. For $\relRestrReflSup$, the frame proofs construct $\relRestrReflBelowSup$ before $\relRestrReflAboveSup$, while the painting proofs proceed in the reverse order. The ordinary commutation-layer steps give hexagons. The cancellation family $\relRestrReflBelowId$ gives a square with one step on one side and three on the other. The full--frame painting case of $\relReflAboveAbove$ uses a compatibility proof supplied alongside the family $L$. This proof equates two composites of supplied degeneracies over the corresponding frame equality. The data for the $\relReflRefl$ laws comprise the families in Tables~\ref{tab:coind} and~\ref{tab:core} together with these compatibility proofs.

\subsubsection{Last-direction degeneracies}
\label{sec:last-direction}

The construction simplifies when only the last--direction degeneracy is supplied at each level. At level $n$, the inserted direction is $n$, so a prefix of height $p$ uses the Below operation $\reflfbelow[n][p][][n-p]$. The prefix reaches the inserted direction only at the full frame, where the Above index is $0$. Writing
\begin{equation*}
  \reflff[n]\defeq\reflfbelow[n][n][][0]
\end{equation*}
its boundary is
\begin{equation*}
  \reflfabove[n][n][][0][d=\d,c=x]
  = (\reflfbelow[n][n][][0][d=\d],\lambda\epsilon.\,x)
  = (\reflff[n][d=\d],\lambda\epsilon.\,x).
\end{equation*}
The supplied cell operation is thus $L(0)$, with target $X_{n+1}(\reflff[n][d=\d],\lambda\epsilon.\,x)$. Its boundary can be constructed using earlier last--direction degeneracies alone: each existing layer is transformed using the last--direction painting operation one level lower, and the constant layer is appended at the end.

Only two face--degeneracy coherence families are needed for this construction. The specialization of $\relRestrReflBelowInf$ makes the layer recursion well--typed, and that of $\relRestrReflBelowId$ makes the final constant layer well--typed. There is no restriction direction after the inserted one, so no $\relRestrReflSup$ case arises. The general $\relReflRefl$ law also involves a degeneracy that is not in the last direction of its source, and therefore is not a law expressible using only these supplied operations.

In the cubical case, compatible coordinate symmetries allow every degeneracy to be expressed using the last--direction degeneracy~\cite{grandis03}. Writing $\varepsilon_i$ for degeneracy in absolute direction $i$ and $\tau_i$ for the exchange of directions $i$ and $i+1$, we have
\begin{equation*}
  \varepsilon_i
  =\tau_i\circ\tau_{i+1}\circ\cdots\circ\tau_{n-1}\circ\varepsilon_n
  \qquad(0\leq i\leq n),
\end{equation*}
where the composite of exchanges is empty when $i=n$. One first inserts the last direction and then moves it to position $i$ by adjacent exchanges. The construction presented above supplies degeneracies in every direction without requiring these symmetries.
