\begin{abstract}
  In an exploration of building simplicial and cubical set models in indexed form, we extend a previous construction of semi--simplicial and semi--cubical sets with degeneracies. The degeneracies we construct can be characterized as adding a new dimension, and coincide with usual degeneracies in the cubical set case. As necessitated by the construction, we refined the degeneracies and coherence conditions outlined in Grandis--Mauri~\cite{grandis03} to make it step--wise. Our work includes a mechanically checked formalization in Rocq.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\subsection{Motivation}
Semi--simplicial sets are usually defined as presheaves from the semi--simplicial category, termed as the \emph{fibred} form~\cite{rourkesanderson71}. In our previous work, we proposed a uniform construction of semi--simplicial and semi--cubical sets using iterated parametericity, termed as the \emph{indexed} form~\cite{hr25}, using the \emph{fibred-indexed correspondence}. That our indexed construction is equivalent to the fibred one is proved in~\cite{Laroche26}. It was originally suggested at Univalent Foundations IAS 2012~\cite{UFwiki2012} that the open problem of constructing semi--simplicial \emph{types} could be solved with the indexed construction, as it supplies more definitional equalities for tackling the combinatorial problem of solving coherence conditions~\footnote{It was originally postulated that the coherences would come for free with the indexed construction, but this is not the case}.

\subsection{Contributions}
We previously exploited the observation that semi--simplicial and semi--cubical sets are naturally unary and binary cases of iterated parametricity~\cite{hr25}. We extend this construction with operations that insert a new coordinate axis: the usual cubical degeneracies in the binary case and their unary analogue~\cite{bernardy15,bernardy12}. The coherence conditions we used to complete our construction made explicit the ones outlined in Grandis--Mauri~\cite{grandis03}. Our work includes a mechanically-checked formalization in Rocq~\href{https://github.com/artagnon/bonak}{\linkicon}, including a proof of equivalence with Grandis--Mauri's definition of cubical sets with degeneracies~\footnote{LLM technology was used as a mechanical automation tool in both the formalization and the paper; in particular, the third author (Alice Laroche) did not make use of the technology}.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$\nu$}{nu}-shape sets}

The categories of augmented semi-simplices and semi-cubes can both be seen as free monoidal categories over natural numbers generated either by elementary faces $\partial_i$ in the augmented semi-simplicial case~\cite{grandis01} or $\partial_i^+$ and $\partial_i^-$ in the semi-cubical case~\cite{grandis03,buchholtz17}. Alternatively, morphisms from $p$ to $n$ in these categories can be represented respectively as inclusions of $\{1\}^p$ to $\{1\}^p$~\footnote{equivalently, as strictly monotone functions from $[1;p]$ to $[1;n]$~\cite{maclane1998categories}} or as inclusions of $\{+,-\}^p$ in $\{+,-\}^n$~\cite{Fahrenberg_2024}. The definitions of categories of augmented semi-simplices and semi-cubes can be seen as only differing in arity $\nu$, used as the face label: the unary case uses a single label, and the binary case uses two labels.

\begin{definition}[$\hexagon$, \cite{hr25}]
  The definition of $\nu$--semi--shape category is shown below. It characterises an inclusion from $\nu^p$ to $\nu^n$ as a word of size $n$ over alphabet $\nu \sqcup \{\kstar\}$ such that a $\kstar$ denotes a retained coordinate and a label in $\nu$ denotes an inserted constant.
  Note that, if $g \circ f$ is well--defined, then the length of $f$ is less than or equal to that of $g$. We write $|l|_{\kstar}$ for the number of stars in a word $l$ and $\epsilon$ for the empty word. Its objects, morphisms, and identities are:
  \begin{align*}
    \Obj_{\hexagon}       & := \Nat                                                                       \\
    \Hom_{\hexagon}(p, n) & := \{l \in (\nu \sqcup \{\kstar\})^n \mid |l|_{\kstar} = p\}                  \\
    g \circ f             & :=
    \begin{cases}
      f                & \text{if $g = \epsilon$}                                                   \\
      a\,(g' \circ f)  & \text{if $g = a\,g'$}, \text{where $a \in \nu$}                            \\
      a\,(g' \circ f') & \text{if $g = \kstar\,g'$, $f = a\,f'$, where $a \in \nu$ or $a = \kstar$} \\
    \end{cases}       \\
    \id                   & := \kstar \ldots \kstar \text{ $n$ times for $\id \in \Hom_{\hexagon}(n, n)$}
  \end{align*}
\end{definition}

In particular, the $1$--semi--shape category presents the augmented semi--simplicial category~\footnote{with a shift by $1$, that is the object $0$ here is the object $-1$ of the augmented semi--simplicial category} while the $2$--semi--shape category presents the semi--cubical category~\footnote{note that this also relates to unary and binary parametricity~\cite{bernardy15, reynolds83}}.

Degeneracies can be added to the $\nu$--semi--shape category in different ways. We here focus on the style of degeneracies defined in Grandis and Mauri for cubical sets~\cite{grandis03} and to their adaptation to the unary case as in Parametric Type Theory~\cite{bernardy15}. We call $\nu$--shape category the corresponding category. There, a morphism from $p$ to $n$ retains $k$ of the $p$ input coordinates: it first discards the other $p-k$ coordinates, then degenerates $n-k$ times the result to obtain $n$ output coordinates.

\begin{definition}[$\hexagon_d$]
  The $\nu$--shape category extends the $\nu$--semi--shape category with degeneracies. A morphism is represented by a pair $(r,f)$ consisting of a selection mask $r$ and a face word $f$. In the mask, a star retains an input coordinate and a bullet discards it. We write $|w|_{\kstar}$ for the number of stars in a word $w$.

  To compose $(r,f):p\to m$ with $(s,g):m\to n$, we first normalize the face word $f$ followed by the mask $s$ using $\mathsf{cross}$. Its inputs have the same length. If they contain $k$ and $l$ stars respectively, its output lies in $\Hom_{\hexagon_d}(k,l)$.
  \[
    \begin{aligned}[t]
      \Obj_{\hexagon_d} &:= \Nat \\
      \Hom_{\hexagon_d}(p,n)
        &:= \bigl\{(r,f)\in\{\kstar,\bullet\}^p\times(\nu\sqcup\{\kstar\})^n\mid |r|_{\kstar}=|f|_{\kstar}\bigr\} \\
      \id_n &:= (\kstar^n,\kstar^n) \\
      (s,g)\circ_{\hexagon_d}(r,f)
        &:= (r\circ_{\hexagon_1}t,\;g\circ_{\hexagon}h) \\
        &\quad\text{where }(t,h)=\mathsf{cross}(f,s)
    \end{aligned}
    \qquad
    \begin{aligned}[t]
      \mathsf{cross}(\epsilon,\epsilon)  &:= (\epsilon,\epsilon) \\
      \mathsf{cross}(a f,\bullet s)      &:= (t,h)               \\
      \mathsf{cross}(a f,\kstar s)       &:= (t,a h)             \\
      \mathsf{cross}(\kstar f,\bullet s) &:= (\bullet t,h)       \\
      \mathsf{cross}(\kstar f,\kstar s)  &:= (\kstar t,\kstar h)
    \end{aligned}
  \]
  Here $\epsilon$ is the empty word, $a\in\nu$, and $(t,h)=\mathsf{cross}(f,s)$ in each recursive clause. The recursion discards a constant when the mask has a bullet and retains it when the mask has a star. An input variable is retained exactly when the mask retains its position.

  Composition then substitutes $t$ into the stars of $r$ and $h$ into the stars of $g$. The first substitution uses $\circ_{\hexagon_1}$, the semi--shape word composition with $\bullet$ as its sole label, and the second uses $\circ_{\hexagon}$. These operations satisfy the identity and associativity laws.
\end{definition}

In particular, a $2$--shape category presents the cubical category, while $1$--shape category presents the augmented simplicial category with cubical--style degeneracies, which we explain in more detail in the next section. We call $n$-$\nu$-shape the object $n$ of the $\nu$-category, together with the morphisms out of it.

\subsection{Parametric degeneracies vs connecting degeneracies}
\label{sec:dgn-flavors}

Despite a common terminology, degeneracies in simplicial sets~\cite{fri08} and in cubical sets~\cite{grandis03} are of a different nature. Degeneracies in cubical sets degenerate an existing $n$-$\nu$-shape along a new axis while degeneracies in simplicial sets degenerate an existing $n$-$\nu$-shape by duplicating an existing axis. In particular, degenerating a $n$-$\nu$-shape $c$ in a cubical set is about \emph{inserting a new axis} $i \leq n$, yielding a $(n+1)$-shape lying on $c$ in the new $i$-th axis and lying on the degeneracy of the $j$-th axis of $c$ in the $j$-th axis (resp. $(j-1)$-th axis) for $j < i$ (resp $j > i$). In the simplicial case, the degeneracy process is better understood by reasoning in augmented simplicial sets~\footnote{where we now shift everything down by $1$ so that the dimension starts at $-1$}: degenerating a $n$-shape $c$ in simplicial sets is about \emph{duplicating along an existing axis} $i < n$, yielding a $(n+1)$-shape lying on $c$ in both the $i$ and $i+1$ axes, while it lies again on the degeneracy of the $j$-th axis (resp. $(j-1)$-th) of $c$ in the $j$-th axis for $j < i$ (resp. $j > i+1$).

We call the axis-duplicating operation a \emph{connecting degeneracy} and the axis-inserting operation \emph{parametric degeneracy}. The unary case of parametric degeneracies have already been considered in the literature, e.g. implicitly in type theories with internal parametricity~\cite{moulin16,bernardy12} and more explicitly in Parametric Type Theory~\cite{bernardy15} where an interpretation in terms of a unary form of cubical sets is given. The binary case of connecting degeneracies happens to be similar to connections in cubical sets, where it should be noted that for arity $2$, there is one connection for each $\alpha \in \{1,2\}$~\footnote{resulting in the ``conjunctive''/``meet'' and ``disjunctive''/``join'' variants~\cite{grandis03}, or also positive and negative~\cite{BrownHiggins81}}, and these connections additionally need parametric degeneracies to fill the faces on side $\alpha' \neq \alpha$.

In Table~\ref{tab:dgn-flavors}, dimensions start at $-1$ in the unary case and at $0$ in the binary case. In particular, writing $X_{-1}$ for the augmentation set in the unary case, there is a parametric degeneracy $r^{-1}_{-1}(a)$ for any $a:X_{-1}$~\footnote{not explicitly pictured below though}.

\subsection{Fibred-indexed correspondence}

We now work in dependent type theory. A type $A$ is an \emph{h--set} if, for any $x,y:A$ and any two proofs $p,q:x=y$, we have $p=q$. Thus equality between elements of $A$ is proof-irrelevant: this is the uniqueness of identity proofs property for $A$ ($\UIP$). We write $\HSet$ for the universe of h--sets.

We call the structures presented by a presheaf on the $\nu$--semi--shape category \emph{$\nu$--set}. Such presheaf can be equivalently presented as a family of sets with faces and coherence laws~\cite{Laroche26}~\href{https://github.com/artagnon/bonak/blob/master/theories/Equiv/Correspondence.v}{\linkicon}, as pictured for the case $\nu=2$ on the left--hand side of Figure~\ref{fig:fibred-indexed-faces}. They can also be presented in what we call indexed form~\cite{hr25}, as pictured on the right--hand side of Figure~\ref{fig:fibred-indexed-faces}.

For arbitrary arity $\nu$, the indexed presentation is obtained from the presheaf presentation by iterating the equivalence between maps and families:
\[
  (\Sigma A : \HSet. A \rightarrow B) \simeq (B \rightarrow \HSet)
\]

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Indexed $\nu$--sets}{Indexed nu-sets}}
\label{sec:faces}

We recall the indexed representation of $\nu$--sets~\cite{hr25,hr26}\href{https://github.com/artagnon/bonak/tree/master/theories/%BDSet/%BDSet.v}{\linkicon}. A \emph{layer} groups the face data for one direction, with one component for each label in $\nu$. A \emph{frame} records a prefix of these boundary layers; a \emph{full frame} contains all of them. A \emph{painting} over a frame supplies the remaining boundary layers and a cell over the completed boundary.

At construction level $n$, we first specify a boundary and then consider the cells that fill it. The earlier families $X_0,\ldots,X_{n-1}$ determine the type of full frames, each consisting of $n$ layers; for each full frame $d$, the next family supplies the h--set $X_n(d)$ of cells with that boundary. Level $n$ corresponds to geometric dimension $n-1$ in the augmented unary case and to dimension $n$ in the binary case.

Let $p\leq n$ be the prefix height and $k:=n-p$ the number of remaining layers. We write $\framep[n][p]$ for frames of height $p$, $\layer[n][p][][d=d]$ for the next layer over a frame $d$, and $\painting[n][p][][d=d]$ for its paintings. A full frame has type $\fullframe[n]=\framep[n][n]$, so $k=0$; its paintings are cells of $X_n(d)$. A successor frame is a dependent pair $(d,l)$ of a frame and its next layer. A painting with layers left to supply is a dependent pair $(l,c)$ of its next layer and remaining painting.

Figure~\ref{fig:cube-prefixes} illustrates the prefixes for $n=3$ and $\nu=2$. The first layer supplies the left and right face paintings. The next adds the bottom and top face paintings over the edges already supplied by the first layer. The final layer adds the front and back face paintings over their completed boundaries, yielding the full frame. Thus each new layer uses the boundary data in the preceding prefix. A painting over the first prefix supplies the remaining four faces, compatible with the two already supplied, together with a cube cell filling the completed boundary. Over the full frame, a painting supplies only the cube cell.

At level $n$, let $i\leq n$ be the chosen insertion direction. When $p\leq i$, the operation \emph{Below} ($\downarrow$) uses the relative direction index $q=i-p\leq k$. When $i\leq p$, the operation \emph{Above} ($\uparrow$) uses $q=p-i\leq p$. The boundary $i=p$ is the common base case, where both indices are $0$.

Below transforms the existing prefix without inserting the repeated layer. It acts on a frame alone and preserves the prefix height:
\begin{equation*}
  \reflfbelow[n][p][][q]:\framep[n][p]\rightarrow\framep[n+1][p]
  \qquad(q\leq k)
\end{equation*}
Above inserts the repeated layer, so it needs the painting $c$ completing the original prefix and produces one more layer:
\begin{equation*}
  \reflfabove[n][p][][q]:\Pi d:\framep[n][p].\,
  \painting[n][p][][d=\d]\rightarrow\framep[n+1][p+1]
  \qquad(q\leq p)
\end{equation*}

Suppose the construction has reached level $n$, with cell families $X_0,\ldots,X_n$ and degeneracy data $R$ below level $n$. Once the next cell family $X_{n+1}$ is chosen, the cell degeneracy $L$ at level $n$ has type
\begin{equation*}
  L:\Pi q\leq n.\,\Pi d:\fullframe[n].\,\Pi x:X_n(d).\,
  X_{n+1}(\reflfabove[n][n][][q][d=\d,c=x])
\end{equation*}

\subsection{Coherence conditions}
\label{sec:coherences}

The five relations of Grandis--Mauri~\cite{grandis03} describe commutation of restrictions and degeneracies, and cancellation of a degeneracy by a restriction in its inserted direction. Grandis--Mauri state the relations between maps in the cubical site. Here we display their images under a contravariant presheaf, which reverses composition. Thus $\delta_r^\alpha$ denotes restriction on cells and $\varepsilon_q$ denotes degeneracy, with face labels $\alpha,\beta\in\nu$.
\begin{equation*}
  \begin{array}{r@{\;}c@{\;}c@{}l@{\qquad}l@{\qquad}l}
    \delta_q^\alpha\delta_r^\beta     & =       &                    &
    \delta_r^\beta\delta_{q+1}^\alpha & r\leq q & (\relRestrRestr)                                                                 \\[0.4em]
                                      &         & \ldelim\{{3}{*}    &
    \varepsilon_{q-1}\delta_r^\alpha  & r<q     & (\relRestrReflInf)                                                               \\
    \delta_r^\alpha\varepsilon_q      & =       &                    &
    \id                               & r=q     & (\relRestrReflId)                                                                \\
                                      &         &                    & \varepsilon_q\delta_{r-1}^\alpha & r>q & (\relRestrReflSup) \\[0.4em]
    \varepsilon_q\varepsilon_r        & =       &                    &
    \varepsilon_{r+1}\varepsilon_q    & q\leq r & (\relReflRefl)
  \end{array}
\end{equation*}
These are zero-based absolute direction indices, ranging over the instances in which the composites are defined. At level $n$, the cell component of $\varepsilon_i$ is supplied by $L(n-i)$. The three cases distinguish a restriction before, at, or after the inserted direction.

The mixed relation above is displayed in the simplifying direction. In the construction tables, the equality proofs are oriented in the direction used for transport, as in the formalization. Coherence names list the operations on the right--hand side of those construction--oriented equations, with the outer operation first. In particular, $\relRestrReflBelowId$ proves $d=\cRF{q}{\alpha}{\cSF{q}{d}}$, reversing the cancellation equation above.

In the indexed construction, these relations become equalities of partial boundaries. The Below/Above distinction refines $\relRestrReflSup$ into $\relRestrReflBelowSup$ and $\relRestrReflAboveSup$, and $\relReflRefl$ into $\relReflBelowBelow$, $\relReflBelowAbove$, and $\relReflAboveAbove$. Together with $\relRestrReflBelowInf$ and $\relRestrReflBelowId$, these are the seven degeneracy coherence families. The underlying $\nu$--set already supplies $\relRestrRestr$.

Typing the layer operations requires transporting paintings along frame equalities. The Below layer step uses $\relRestrReflBelowInf$ to commute degeneracy with restriction. At Above index $0$, $\relRestrReflBelowId$ identifies the restriction of the degenerate frame with the original frame, allowing the input painting to form a constant layer. At a successor Above index, the layer step uses $\relRestrReflAboveSup$. The restriction layer similarly uses $\relRestrRestr$ to commute two restrictions. The frame operations and the types of the coherences they require are therefore constructed together.

\subsection{Last-direction degeneracies}
\label{sec:last-direction}

The construction simplifies when only the last--direction degeneracy is supplied at each level. At level $n$, the inserted direction is $n$, so a prefix of height $p$ uses the Below operation $\reflfbelow[n][p][][k]$. The prefix reaches the inserted direction only at the full frame, where the Above index is $0$. Writing
\begin{equation*}
  \reflff[n]\defeq\reflfbelow[n][n][][0]
\end{equation*}
its boundary is
\begin{equation*}
  \reflfabove[n][n][][0][d=\d,c=x]
  = (\reflfbelow[n][n][][0][d=\d],\refllabove[n][n][][0][d=\d,c=x])
  = (\reflff[n][d=\d],\refllabove[n][n][][0][d=\d,c=x])
\end{equation*}
The supplied cell operation is thus $L(0)$, with target $X_{n+1}(\reflff[n][d=\d],\refllabove[n][n][][0][d=\d,c=x])$. Its boundary can be constructed using earlier last--direction degeneracies alone: each existing layer is transformed using the last--direction painting operation one level lower, and the constant layer is appended at the end.

Only two face--degeneracy coherence families are needed for this construction. The specialization of $\relRestrReflBelowInf$ makes the layer recursion well--typed, and that of $\relRestrReflBelowId$ makes the final constant layer well--typed. There is no restriction direction after the inserted one, so no $\relRestrReflSup$ case arises. The general $\relReflRefl$ law also involves a degeneracy that is not in the last direction of its source, and therefore is not a law expressible using only these supplied operations.

In the cubical case, compatible coordinate symmetries allow every degeneracy to be expressed using the last--direction degeneracy~\cite{grandis03}. Writing $\varepsilon_i$ for degeneracy in absolute direction $i$ and $sigma_i$ for the exchange of directions $i$ and $i+1$, we have
\begin{equation*}
  \varepsilon_i
  =\sigma_i\circ\sigma_{i+1}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma_{n-1}\circ\varepsilon_n
  \qquad(i\leq n)
\end{equation*}
where the composite of exchanges is empty when $i=n$. One first inserts the last direction and then moves it to position $i$ by adjacent exchanges. The construction presented above supplies degeneracies in every direction without requiring these symmetries.
